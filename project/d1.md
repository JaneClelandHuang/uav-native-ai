# Project Deliverable 1 — Project Vision

## Objectives

Before your team writes any code, decide what you are building and what
success will look like. D1 is the first state of your team's project
website.

By the end of this deliverable, your team will have:

- Chosen a disaster-response problem and a mission for your UAV swarm.
- Written a vision statement: who the system is for, what problem it
  solves, and why it is better than what exists.
- Written one use case in Alistair Cockburn's fully dressed format,
  including the extensions where things go wrong.
- Sketched wireframes of what the person supervising the swarm needs to
  see.
- Defined a small set of success criteria that could actually fail.

**Native AI focus — Thinking Before Generating.** When Claude can build
almost anything in an afternoon, the scarce skill is knowing what to build
and how you'll know it worked. Each D1 artifact gets reused later: the use
case becomes your final demo script and your first end-to-end test, the
wireframes become the spec for your GUI, and the success criteria become
the oracles for your D4 testing agent.

### What you build

#### 1. Vision statement

One paragraph, no jargon. Say who the system is for, what problem they
have, what your system does about it, and how it differs from what they
use today. A template that works (from Geoffrey Moore):

> **For** *the people you serve* **who** *the problem they have*, **the**
> *system's name* **is a** *what kind of thing it is* **that** *its key
> capability*. **Unlike** *today's alternative*, **it** *what makes it
> different*.

Add a short **final demonstration vision**: what will we see your system
do on Dec. 8?

Your project must have the course's required shape: **multiple UAVs** and
an operational **Perceive → Reason → Act** loop, with a human meaningfully
involved. The vision should make it clear where each of these comes in.

#### 2. One use case, Cockburn fully dressed

Write **one user-goal use case**: one primary actor achieving one goal in
one sitting. Include every field:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Use case | ID and a short active-verb goal, e.g. *UC-1 Supervise drones at a remote site* |
| Context of use | A sentence or two on the situation |
| Scope · level | Your system, treated as a black box · user goal |
| Primary actor | Who wants something from the system |
| Secondary actors | Other people or systems the use case relies on |
| Stakeholders and interests | Everyone who cares about the outcome, and what each needs protected |
| Preconditions | What must be true before it starts |
| Minimal guarantees | What stays true even when it fails |
| Success guarantees | What is true when it succeeds |
| Trigger | What starts it |
| Main success scenario | 3–9 numbered steps, each saying who acts: *Operator…*, *System…* |
| Extensions | What can go wrong at a step (2a, 3a…, or \*a for any step), and what happens then, as numbered sub-steps |
| Open issues | What your team hasn't decided yet |

Two rules for the steps:

- **No UI detail.** Write "Operator approves the response", not "Operator
  clicks the green button". The screens belong in the wireframes.
- **Make the loop visible.** A reader should be able to point at the
  steps where the system perceives, where it reasons (and where AI is
  involved), where it acts, and where the human steps in.

Expect the extensions to be longer than the main scenario. That is where
most of the engineering is.

#### 3. Wireframes

Sketch the screens the person supervising the swarm needs for your use
case, one per key screen (typically two or three). Boxes, labels and
placeholder text, no polish: paper, a whiteboard or simple shapes are all
fine. Photograph or export them for the website.

Label each region with the use-case step or extension it supports. Every
step where the human sees or decides something needs a home on a screen,
and so do the extensions that the human has to notice.

#### 4. Success criteria

**3–5 measurable outcomes** that would convince a skeptic your project
worked. For each one, give:

- what success looks like,
- how it is measured (a number, a threshold, a count of trials),
- where it comes from: your vision, a use-case step, or a guarantee.

If nothing could fail it, it isn't a criterion yet. "Operators will find
the system intuitive" fails that test; "an operator approves or rejects a
proposed response within 10 s in 9 of 10 trials" passes it.

#### 5. What changed after Thursday

A short list on the website: the most useful questions or criticisms from
Thursday, and what you changed because of them (or why you didn't).

### Deliverable

On your team's project website. Your team chooses, unanimously, how to host it: a public repo
with GitHub Pages (built from `docs/`), a separately hosted site, or Markdown in `docs/` of a
private repo. Whatever you choose, put the link to your current site at the top of your team
repo's README, and make sure the instructor and your partner team can open it. If your repo is
public, keep `.env` and API keys out of it, including its history.

The layout below assumes the site lives in your team repo; adapt it if yours doesn't:

```text
<team-repo>/
├── index.md              Home: project name, team members, one-line vision,
│                         link to Vision & Use Cases
└── vision/
    ├── index.md          vision statement, final demo vision, success criteria,
    │                     what changed after Thursday, who did what
    ├── uc-1.md           your use case, fully dressed
    └── wireframes/       images, shown in uc-1.md or index.md
```

```bash
git add index.md vision
git commit -m "D1: project vision"
git push
```

Then open the link in your README and check that the pages render there.

### Before You Submit

Be ready to explain:

- Who your primary actor is, and why that person and not someone else.
- Which steps of your use case are Perceive, Reason and Act, and where
  the human can say no.
- The extension you think is most likely to happen in the real world, and
  what your system does about it.
- How you would measure each success criterion, and what result would
  mean the project failed.

> **Know what you're building, and how you'll know it worked.**
