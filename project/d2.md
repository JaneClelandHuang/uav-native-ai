## Objectives

Turn your D1 vision into a credible architecture, and use evidence to
settle the one architectural decision you can't make on opinion alone.

By the end of this deliverable, your team will have:

- An architecture you can walk your use case through, with a clear
  responsibility for every component.
- Your most significant architectural decisions recorded as short
  architecture decision records (ADRs).
- Run a time-boxed architectural spike, and changed (or confirmed) your
  architecture because of what it showed.
- Set up your team's pull-request workflow, including a PR-review skill
  every teammate's Claude can use.

**Native AI focus — AI for Throwaway Code, Humans for the Evidence.** A
spike is where generated code pays off most: it exists only to answer a
question, and you throw it away. The numbers it produces are different.
Someone on your team checks every one before it goes in D2.

### What you build

#### 1. Architecture

- **System context:** what is inside your system and what isn't (the
  course infrastructure, operators, other systems).
- **Architecture sketch:** components and the connectors between them.
  A cleaned-up version of Thursday's sketch is fine. Show where your
  system perceives, reasons and acts, and where the human sees, decides
  and can say no.
- **Responsibilities:** one sentence per component.
- **Interfaces and data flows:** the MQTT topics (or other connectors)
  each component publishes and subscribes to, with one example message
  for each new topic.
- **Walk-through:** your D1 use case traced across the sketch, step by
  step, including at least one extension.
- **Quality goals:** the 2–3 qualities that matter most for your system
  (for example responsiveness, safety, scalability to more drones), and
  where the architecture addresses each.

#### 2. Architectural decisions as ADRs

Record your **3–5 most significant decisions** as ADRs in Nygard's
format, each about a page or less:

| Part | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Title | Numbered, a short noun phrase: *ADR-1 Central planner on the ground station* |
| Status | Proposed, accepted, or superseded (by which ADR) |
| Context | The forces at play: what you needed, what constrained you |
| Decision | What you decided, stated plainly |
| Consequences | What becomes easier, what becomes harder, what you now have to live with |

Your spike's decision should be one of them.

#### 3. Architectural spike

A small, time-boxed experiment (about **6 team-hours**) that answered one
architectural question with evidence. Write it up in six parts:

| Part | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Question | One sentence, naming what you measured |
| Why it matters | Which architectural decision depended on the answer |
| Options | What you expected, and what you would do under each outcome |
| Experiment | The setup, how long it took, and whether the time box held |
| Evidence | Numbers, plots, logs or a short recording, **with the sample behind them** (how many runs, frames, flights) |
| Decision | What you decided, and how it changed your architecture (or why it didn't) |

The spike code lives in `spikes/<name>/` in your team repo and is never
merged into your system. A spike that answered "no, that won't work" is a
success.

#### 4. Pull-request workflow and PR-review skill

Your team repo came with a PR template and the team rules in `CLAUDE.md`.
This week your git history shows you working through them:

- Your D3 vertical slice is broken into **GitHub issues** on a project
  board.
- **At least 2 merged PRs per team member** between Tuesday, Nov. 10 and
  Monday, Nov. 16, each approved by a teammate other than its author.
- **No direct commits to `main`.**
- **Small, focused PRs:** each one closes one issue, and most change fewer
  than about 300 lines. Big-bang PRs don't count toward the minimum.
- The **"Our project" section of `CLAUDE.md`** is filled in: components,
  topics, how to run the system, conventions.
- A **PR-review skill** is committed at
  `.claude/skills/pr-review/SKILL.md`.

The skill reviews a pull request before a teammate does, the way we
reviewed PRs with Claude in the Patches lesson. Scaffold it with
`skill-creator`, as in HW5. Given a PR, it should:

1. Read the PR description and the diff (`gh pr view`, `gh pr diff`).
2. Check that the template is filled in and the PR closes an issue.
3. Check the change against your architecture in `CLAUDE.md`: is it in
   the component that owns this responsibility, using the topics you
   documented?
4. Check that tests were added or updated, or that the PR says why not.
5. Flag any `.env`, key or secret in the diff.
6. Draft review comments for the human reviewer, including the lines the
   author should be ready to explain.

It must **never approve or merge** a PR. A teammate approves.

#### 5. What changed after Tuesday

A short list on your website: the most useful questions or criticisms
from Tuesday, and what you changed because of them (or why you didn't).

### Spike gallery (Tuesday, Nov. 10)

No slide talks this time. Each team runs a **station** where visitors can
look at your evidence and argue with it.

**Your station** is a laptop (or two) showing, in this order:

1. **Architecture:** your sketch, with your use case walked across it.
2. **Spike:** the question, the experiment, and the **evidence** — numbers,
   a plot, a log, or a short recording — with the sample behind it.
3. **Decision:** what you decided, and your architecture before and after.

A visitor should be able to get the question, the evidence and the
decision in about **2 minutes**. The rest of the visit is conversation.

**How it runs:** three rounds of about 12 minutes. In each round one
member of your team stays at your station and the others visit another
station; you rotate who stays, so **everyone staffs the station at least
once** and everyone visits. Over the three rounds every team visits every
other station. When you visit your **partner team**, you bring its
prepared questions: does the evidence support the decision, was the
sample big enough, what if the answer had come back the other way?

The instructor visits every station.

**Before class on Nov. 10:** link whatever your station shows (slides,
a page, a notebook) from your website.
